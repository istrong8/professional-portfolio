Moon and Mars

How do you show a reactor is safe when the nearest people are astronauts, the ground shakes from moonquakes, and there is no air to carry anything away?

This page tracks my research and standards work on fission power for the lunar and Martian surface: the questions, the projects, and where each one stands.

§ 01

Two destinations, two safety problems

Terrestrial safety methods assume a public at a fence line, an atmosphere, and earthquakes. Neither the Moon nor Mars offers all three, so each method has to be rebuilt from its physics.

The Moon

  • 1/6 g
  • No atmosphere
  • ~40–390 K
  • Moonquakes
  1. What should a safety basis protect when the only people nearby are the crew?
  2. How do hazard criteria change when earthquakes become moonquakes, and wind becomes micrometeoroids and landing-plume ejecta?
  3. How does released material move in a vacuum, where nothing carries it downwind?

Mars

  • 0.38 g
  • ~6 mbar CO₂
  • Dust storms
  • 3–22 min light delay
  1. Is a fission reactor feasible for a crewed Mars surface base, and what does its safety case need to show?
  2. How do a thin atmosphere and dust storms change heat rejection and the spread of released material?
  3. How does a reactor protect itself when Earth is minutes away?

§ 02

Projects

Independent academic work at Idaho State University. Each states its status plainly.

In development

Path to Moon SAR

A worked example of how a lunar surface reactor could move through a safety design sequence, from strategy to a preliminary safety analysis, using DOE reactor safety methods adapted for the lunar surface.

  • Safety basis
  • Hazard & accident analysis

Paper in progress

Natural phenomena hazards beyond Earth

How seismic, wind, and temperature hazard criteria translate to the lunar and Martian surface, as groundwork for a future standard.

  • Moonquakes
  • Meteoroids
  • Dust

v0.2 · Open source

TLECUIL — fission surface power sizer

A first-order sizing tool for surface reactor power systems, with a commented Python engine, an interactive calculator, and a theory document written for undergraduates. Named for tlecuilli, the three-stone hearth.
