Space fission safety · Research overview

Moon and Mars

How do you show a reactor is safe when the nearest people are astronauts, the ground shakes from moonquakes, and there is no air to carry anything away?

The first lunar reactor is planned for the south pole, where the Sun circles the horizon and some ground never sees daylight. This page tracks my research and standards work on fission power for the lunar and Martian surface: the questions, the projects, and where each one stands.

§ 01

Two destinations, two safety problems

Terrestrial safety methods assume a public at a fence line, an atmosphere, and earthquakes. Neither the Moon nor Mars offers all three, so each method has to be rebuilt from its physics.

The Moon

  • 1/6 g
  • No atmosphere
  • ~40–390 K
  • Moonquakes
  1. What should a safety basis protect when the only people nearby are the crew?
  2. How do hazard criteria change when earthquakes become moonquakes, and wind becomes micrometeoroids and landing-plume ejecta?
  3. How does released material move in a vacuum, where nothing carries it downwind?

§ 02

Lunar hazards, by the numbers

Meteoroids and moonquakes are the Moon's version of wind and earthquakes. No sensor reports them live today, so these tools compute them from the published models and the Apollo record.

Model: Grün et al. (1985) interplanetary flux at 1 AU on a randomly oriented plate, 20 km/s mean speed, as used in NASA's Meteoroid Engineering Model. Expected hits = flux × area × years; chance = 1 − e−N. LRO counted about 33% more new craters than the standard lunar model predicted, and secondary debris from nearby impacts adds slower particles, so treat these as a floor.

Typical activity windows and peaks from the International Meteor Organization calendar; peaks shift by about a day from year to year. Showers add only about 1–5% of the total meteoroid flux, depending on size, so they matter less than the steady background in the calculator above.
