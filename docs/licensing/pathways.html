Licensing pathways

The routes a new U.S. reactor or fuel-cycle facility can take, what each one requires under the CFR and DOE directives, and how long each step is supposed to take.

Current project status is on the tracker.

§ 01

Licensing vs regulation

Regulation

The whole system of control: the rules themselves (10 CFR, DOE Orders and Standards), the guidance that interprets them, and the inspection, oversight and enforcement that continue for the life of a facility. It applies to everyone in a class of activity, whether or not they hold a license yet.

Licensing

One step inside regulation: the agency reviews a specific applicant's design, site and programs against those rules and issues a permit, license or approval with conditions. A license is a document with a docket number; regulation is what the licensee lives under afterward (inspections, reporting, amendments, renewal).

Where DOE fits

The NRC licenses commercial facilities. DOE does not license; it authorizes its own and its partners' facilities under the Atomic Energy Act, with 10 CFR 830 as the binding rule and an approved safety basis (DSA/TSR) instead of a license. DOE-authorized facilities are exempt from NRC licensing, which is why a commercial follow-on still needs the NRC.

§ 02

Regulatory routes

Four routes exist for a new U.S. reactor in late 2026. Test and demonstration units increasingly go through DOE first, then use the new NRC "bridge" rule to bring that evidence into a commercial application.

DOE authorization

DOE-STD-1271-2025 · 10 CFR 830 Subparts A & B · EO 14301 Reactor Pilot Program → Nuclear Energy Launch Pad
  1. OTA signed with DOE (formal partnership).
  2. NSDA: design requirements, safety-analysis approach, Code of Record, regulatory engagement.
  3. PDSA at about 50% design: hazard and accident analysis, safety SSCs.
  4. DSA + TSRs at final design; procedures and training complete.
  5. Readiness review → DOE startup approval (Secretary of Energy for new reactors).
  6. Startup under a Joint Test Group → criticality → power operations.

DOE review target: 45 days from formal acceptance of a safety-basis document. Reactor DSA method: DOE-STD-1237-2021. NPH and hazard categorization: NE-STD-1020-2025 and NE-STD-1027-2025. QA: DOE NE Order 414.1 (Aug 2025), ASME NQA-1 preferred for Hazard Category 1–3.

NRC two-step: 10 CFR Part 50

Construction permit (CP) then operating license (OL). Power reactors and non-power test/research reactors.
  1. Pre-application: Regulatory Engagement Plan, white papers, topical reports (methods, codes, QA).
  2. CP application: PSAR (§50.34(a)) + Environmental Report (Part 51). Can be phased, e.g. LWA first.
  3. Docketing, then safety evaluation and EIS or EA/FONSI.
  4. Hearing and Commission decision → CP issued.
  5. Construction under NRC inspection (ARCOP for advanced reactors).
  6. OL application: FSAR (§50.34(b)) → OL → fuel load.

QA: 10 CFR 50 Appendix B; RG 1.28 Rev 6 endorses NQA-1-2017, -2019 and -2022. Prior DOE/DoW evidence: new §50.43(f) (final rule Oct 2026), review targets about 18 months first-of-a-kind, 12 months nth-of-a-kind.

NRC one-step: 10 CFR Part 52

Combined license (COL), standard design approval (SDA), design certification, early site permit (ESP).
  1. Pre-application and topical reports.
  2. COLA, which can be phased (site and environmental first, safety second).
  3. Review, ACRS, hearing → COL.
  4. Construction + ITAAC → §52.103(g) finding → fuel load.

An SDA (Subpart E) lets a design be referenced by later CP, OL or COL applicants. An ESP pre-qualifies a site (TVA Clinch River, 2019).

NRC risk-informed: 10 CFR Part 53

Final rule 91 FR 15696 (30 Mar 2026), effective 29 Apr 2026. Optional, technology-inclusive.
  1. Technology-inclusive safety criteria with risk-informed design flexibility.
  2. Graded security requirements.
  3. Same CP/OL or COL structure; the §50.43(f) bridge rule also amends Part 53.

Not yet final: proposed Part 57, "Licensing Requirements for Microreactors and Other Reactors with Comparable Risk Profiles." Fuel cycle facilities (Parts 40 and 70) are not covered by the bridge rule.

§ 04

Fuel cycle licensing: uranium conversion

Conversion is source material (natural or depleted uranium), so the base rule is 10 CFR Part 40, not the reactor parts. Raven-Flint is the worked example because it uses both an NRC license and a DOE route.

Conversion is source material (natural or depleted uranium), so the base rule is 10 CFR Part 40, not Part 50/53 (reactors) or Part 70 (special nuclear material). Part 70 applies only if the company later handles enriched uranium.

What is not in Part 40. NRC proposed in 2011 to add integrated safety analysis (ISA) requirements for Part 40 facilities holding 2,000 kg or more of UF6 (Subpart H, guidance draft NUREG-1962), then discontinued that rulemaking on 29 Jul 2016. A conversion applicant is not required to file a Part 70-style ISA, though the review still covers accident and chemical hazards. Sure

The §50.43(f) bridge rule covers reactors only, so Torch's DOE data will not get an expedited NRC path for Forge. Sure
